# Drafting

## Overview

Turn intent into a communication that works for the reader.

**Core principle:** Frontload the point. Every draft starts with what the reader needs to know — not with context-building, pleasantries, or preamble. The reader's time matters more than the writer's comfort.

---

## Step 1: Understand the Intent

Before writing anything, be clear on:

1. **What does the writer want the reader to DO or FEEL after reading this?** (act, approve, understand, not panic, etc.)
2. **Who is the reader?** (boss, team, customer, stranger, technical, non-technical)
3. **What's the relationship and tone?** (formal, peer, friendly, sensitive)
4. **Any constraints?** (length limit, deadline urgency, things NOT to say)

If the user gave you the intent in their message, extract these four things. If anything is unclear, ask one question — the most important one — before drafting.

---

## Step 2: Match Format to Context

| Type | Tone guide | Structure |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Formal email** | Professional, no contractions, complete sentences | Subject → greeting → point → context → CTA → close |
| **Informal email** | Conversational, direct, contractions ok | Point → context → next step (no fluff) |
| **Slack / chat** | Casual, short, scannable | One thought per message; bold key info |
| **Announcement** | Clear, inclusive, forward-looking | What → why → what it means for you → next steps |
| **Technical doc section** | Precise, passive voice ok, no filler | What it is → how it works → when to use → gotchas |
| **Apology / sensitive message** | Warm, specific, no hedging | Acknowledge → take responsibility → what changes |

---

## Step 3: Draft Point-First

```
1. Write the most important sentence first (the thing the reader must walk away knowing)
2. Add the minimum context needed to understand it
3. State what you need from the reader (if anything)
4. Close cleanly — no "please don't hesitate to reach out" filler
```

**Test:** Cover everything after the first sentence. Does the first sentence alone do the job? If yes, everything after is supporting material, not core.

---

## Step 4: Edit Pass — Cut the Filler

After drafting, scan for and remove:

| Filler pattern | Replace with |
|----------------|-------------|
| "I hope this email finds you well" | Nothing. Start with the point. |
| "As per my previous email..." | Just say what you need again. |
| "I wanted to reach out because..." | Just reach out. Start with the reason. |
| "Please don't hesitate to contact me" | "Questions? [contact]" or nothing. |
| "It is important to note that..." | Just note it. |
| Passive voice hiding accountability | "We made an error" not "An error was made" |
| Hedging on bad news | State the thing directly, then add context |

---

## Tone Calibration

**Too formal for the context:** Sounds stiff, creates distance, reader feels like they're being addressed, not spoken to.

**Too casual for the context:** Sounds careless, undermines credibility for important messages.

**Right:** Matches the relationship. Reads like a person wrote it for this specific reader.

**When unsure:** Err slightly formal. You can always loosen on revision; sounding unprofessional is harder to recover from.

---

## Sensitive Messages

For apologies, bad news, or conflict:

1. **Don't bury the lead.** Reader will be anxious scanning for the bad news. State it early.
2. **Be specific.** "I'm sorry for the confusion" is not an apology. "I'm sorry I sent the wrong file and caused you to redo the work" is.
3. **Don't over-explain.** Explanation ≠ excuse. One sentence of context is enough.
4. **Say what changes.** An apology without a change is noise.
5. **Don't ask for forgiveness explicitly.** It puts the burden on the reader.

---

## Common Failures

| Failure | What it looks like | Fix |
|---------|--------------------|-----|
| **Burying the lead** | Key info is paragraph 3 | Move it to sentence 1 |
| **Tone mismatch** | Casual email to a formal audience or vice versa | Re-read for the relationship; adjust register |
| **No clear ask** | Reader doesn't know what to do next | Add one explicit CTA: "Can you approve by Friday?" |
| **Over-explaining** | 4 paragraphs of context before the point | Cut context to one sentence; trust the reader |
| **Filler phrases** | "Hope this finds you well" / "Please do not hesitate" | Delete. Always. |
| **Hedging bad news** | "There may have been some issues with..." | Say what happened directly |
| **Writing for the writer** | Includes things that make the writer feel better, not the reader | Ask: does the reader need this sentence? |

---

## Red Flags — Revise Before Sending

- The most important information is not in the first two sentences
- You don't know what you want the reader to do after reading this
- You used "I wanted to" or "I hope" to open
- The message is longer than the situation warrants
- You're softening bad news so much it's unclear there is bad news
- You wrote it for yourself, not the reader
